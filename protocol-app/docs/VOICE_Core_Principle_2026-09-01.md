# The core principle, in Kevin's words

*1 Sep 2026, spoken while getting ready to review the Learn draft. Stored
verbatim (lightly cleaned of transcription stutter, nothing added) because a
transcript sweep confirmed no earlier full version exists anywhere — fragments
of it are scattered through the rulings, but this is the first time the whole
thing was said in one breath. This is a SOURCE document, not shipped copy: when
it becomes cards, law 5 applies (epistemic status travels with each claim).
The thinking is his; the grading comes later.*

---

The key is that we're learning what types of pain are good and what is bad.
The core principle, the main takeaway of this whole app, is to learn to listen
to your body and interpret what it's telling you. Because for so long we're
just taught that pain equals bad. Pain means rest it. Pain means don't do that.

But in reality certain types of pain are actually feedback saying: hey, this
needs your attention. Hey, I've got a muscle locked up over here that's keeping
things out of whack. Hey, your fascia is all bound up and it's restricting your
motion or your breathing.

So you can have that super sensitive bottom of your foot that needs to be
worked out. You're not going to hurt your foot by leaning in and applying
pressure to it — or at least, that's very unlikely. What you're going to do is
break up the fascia and give your foot the freedom to articulate and do what it
was designed to do, before it spent a lifetime locked up in a prison of shoes
that were not designed for your foot to actually move the way it was created
to.

I've learned that in certain places, when I'm leaning into the pain, I'll get
that shooting nerve sensation. That's not necessarily a bad thing. I'm not
saying that's something people should aim for — but what it's telling me is
there's nerve activation, because certain things have gone dormant and gone
offline. Part of this process is restoring things and bringing them back
online — bringing nerve activation to regions where it slowly started to shut
down. That decay isn't permanent. It's not real decay. It just learned that,
hey, this guy doesn't use this range of motion or activate these muscles. So
the nerves to that area go to sleep. They go on vacation. And you've got to
wake them back up and be like: hang on, dude, you're here for a purpose. Let me
remind you what your job is so you can stay active.

And that's kind of the whole protocol. **Learn what the body is telling you.
Do what the body needs as a result of understanding the signaling. Then restore
your body's ability to function the way it was designed, by restoring its
capacity to do what it was designed to do.**

Because when I free up the fascia that lets my belly move, and my ribs, it
gives me access to breathe with my diaphragm — I couldn't do that before. When
I restore the nerve function in my feet and my lower legs, that gives me access
to the stabilizer muscles — I couldn't do that before. I'm teaching my body how
to be a body again. But it starts with me understanding what my body's been
telling me that I've been ignoring the whole time.

Don't be afraid of pain. Not all pain is bad. Some of it is a signal that you
need to lean in and do something about it. You have to be comfortable leaning
into that pain to get to the results on the other side.

## The boundary he drew in the same breath

Same conversation, and it is the other half of the teaching — the part that
keeps the above from being misread as "pain is always an invitation":

> "If I have trouble with the joint because there's a genuine problem — like
> I've torn my ACL and my knee is in pain — that doesn't mean that I need to
> work my knee more. We obviously can't diagnose people, we can't give them bad
> instructions."

So the typology the app teaches has three kinds, not two:

1. **Signal pain** — the locked muscle, the bound fascia, the sensitive foot,
   the dormant zone waking up. Feedback that says lean in.
2. **Structural injury** — the torn ACL. Not an invitation to work it more,
   and not the app's to diagnose. The teaching is that this kind exists and
   reads differently.
3. **The stop signals** (already ruled, R25/law 10) — sharp shooting pain, a
   joint giving way, lightheadedness. Stop and look, every time.

The build already encodes #3's arithmetic side: the "it gave way" tap routes to
stop-and-evaluate and moves no weights — a joint event can never be misread as
"weak muscle, load it more." What today adds is the teaching layer: #1 vs #2 is
a distinction a person learns to make, and teaching it IS the app (his words:
"it starts with me understanding what my body's been telling me").
